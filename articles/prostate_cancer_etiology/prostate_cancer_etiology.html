Prostate Cancer Etiology

Cole Ruoff · September 19, 2026

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men, and there is reported to be 1.5 million new cases and 400,000 deaths attributed to prostate cancer each year1. Due to this widespread impact, much research has been focused on understanding the risk factors, initiation, and progression of prostate cancer. It’s clear that prostate cancer is driven by a multitude of factors ranging from family history to somatic mutations accumulated throughout life. Here, I will review the risk factors, mutations, and epigenetic alterations associated with prostate cancer tumorigenesis.

Risk Factors

Age

There are various risk factors for prostate cancer, and one of the strongest absolute risk factors for developing prostate cancer is age. It’s been reported that incidence of prostate cancer increases logarithmically with age. United States cancer statistics in 2022 showed that prostate cancer risk increases from 1.8% in men aged 60-69 years old to 9% in individuals aged >69 years old2. The overall lifetime risk of prostate cancer was reported to be 12.5%2. The development of prostate cancer is so common that autopsy studies showed that prostate carcinoma lesions were detected in over 50% of men aged >79 years old3.

Family History

Along with age, family history is a major risk factor for prostate cancer. It’s been reported that having one first-degree relative with prostate cancer doubles your individual risk. Having two or more first-degree relatives increases the relative risk to 5-11 times compared to the general population4,5. This non-intervenable risk factor is a reflection of both shared genetic variants and familial screening habits6.

Pathogenic Germline Mutations

Although most cases of prostate cancer are sporadic, there are known pathogenic variants associated with prostate cancer, especially germline mutations. Pathogenic variants include genes such as MUTYH7, which encodes a DNA glycosylase that preserves genome stability8. Also, BRCA1 and BRCA29, known tumor suppressors that work by repairing double stranded breaks in DNA via homologous recombination10,11. Additionally, variants of the ATM gene have been associated with prostate cancer initiation7. ATM encodes a serine/threonine protein kinase that’s activated by DNA double-strand breaks and coordinates cell-cycle checkpoints, DNA repair, and apoptosis12.

Intervenable Risk Factors

There are well established intervenable risk factors associated with prostate cancer such as diet and obesity.

Diet

There are strong data that show lifestyle factors, especially diet, are associated with increased risk of developing prostate cancer and increased risk of prostate cancer progression13,14. Diets resembling the “Western diet,” which are high in saturated fat, processed meats, red meat consumption, and dairy, have been shown to increase risk15. Diets that are more plant-based, which include fruits, vegetables, green tea, and healthy fats, have been shown to have protective effects by lowering your risk15. It’s believed that an individual’s diet modulates risk for developing prostate cancer and disease progression due to its effect on inflammation, oxidative stress, and sex hormones15.

Obesity

Obesity is a major preventable risk factor for various types of cancer1,16,17. Particularly in prostate cancer, obesity is associated with elevated risk of disease recurrence after treatment, disease progression, and prostate cancer mortality17. The physiological mechanisms that link obesity to worse prognosis with prostate cancer are still relatively unknown. Similarly to diet, current evidence points to increased risk stemming from altered insulin and sex hormone concentrations17.

Somatic Mutations

The somatic mutations that are involved in prostate cancer initiation are very diverse and span a wide spectrum. The most well studied somatic mutations are present in the genes TP53, PTEN, and SPOP. Additionally, ETV family gene fusions, like TMPRSS2-ERG, contribute greatly to prostate cancer development and progression1. Prostate cancer has been found to have more frequent copy number alterations (CNAs), displaying a median of 32 per patient, compared to single nucleotide variations (SNVs), which only present 0.42 SNVs/Mbp1.

One of the most common somatic mutations is TP53 mutations, a known tumor suppressor gene18. TP53 is known as the “guardian of the genome” and functions as a transcription factor that maintains genome stability. TP53 maintains genome stability via activating cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, or senescence19. TP53 mutations commonly occur in advanced and metastatic states of prostate cancer, which lead to progression and treatment resistance through impairing genome stability20.

Another common tumor suppressor gene that is frequently altered in prostate cancer is PTEN1. The loss or mutation of PTEN occurs in about 20% of radical prostatectomy specimens and about 50% of castration-resistant specimens1. The alteration of PTEN leads to the activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway, which promotes the phosphorylation, cytoplasmic translocation, and inactivation of FOXO1. This subsequently leads to increased proliferation and invasion21. PTEN is an important biomarker for distinguishing aggressive prostate cancer cases, since it is also highly associated with higher Gleason score, worse prognosis, and neuroendocrine differentiation.

SPOP somatic mutations are one of the most common alterations in primary prostate cancer, reported to occur in 6-15% of cases22. SPOP is an E3 ubiquitin ligase, and one of its substrates is the androgen receptor (AR). As a result, SPOP-mutant prostate cancer cases have higher AR activity compared to non SPOP-mutant cases23.

FOXA1 mutations are also common in prostate cancer. Interestingly, FOXA1 mutations show high variability in prevalence across ethnicities. In Western primary prostate cancer, 4-9% of cases possess a FOXA1 mutation, while African and Asian prostate cancer cases show about 8% and up to 41%, respectively22,24,25. In prostate cancer, FOXA1 acts as a pioneer transcription factor by opening chromatin, which assists in AR binding and subsequent AR signaling26. It has also been found that FOXA1, in cooperation with FOXA2, sustains AR-independent proliferation, enabling AR-negative lineages to progress27.

The chromosomal rearrangement linking two genes, TMPRSS2 and ERG, is present in about 50% of prostate cancer cases, making it the most frequent genetic alteration in prostate cancer28. The mechanism of this gene fusion involves overexpression of ERG driven by androgen response elements. This leads to increased invasion and malignant transformation29.

Epigenetic Alterations

Along with genetic alterations, prostate cancer often displays frequent epigenetic alterations. The occurrence and progression of prostate cancer is likely driven by these alterations, which modulate how specific genes are expressed. Changes in DNA methylation are a common feature of prostate cancer30,31. CpG islands in promoter regions display hypermethylation, which leads to transcriptional silencing of multiple tumor suppressor genes such as GSTP1, APC, and RASSF1A32. Hypermethylation of the GSTP1 promoter may be a useful epigenetic biomarker, as it is detected in about 90% of prostate cancer and about 70% of premalignant lesions called prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN)32. There are also changes in histone modifications in prostate cancer. In castration-resistant prostate cancer, H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 are enriched at AR enhancers, which promotes tumor growth via the upregulation of cell cycle genes33. Also, changes in H3K27me3 modifications are found to silence tumor suppressor genes, leading to tumor growth and progression34. Other forms of epigenetic reprogramming that have been found in prostate cancer include dysregulation of non-coding RNAs and microRNAs35.

Conclusion

The initiation of prostate cancer is multifactorial, involving various risk factors, lifestyle choices, genetic alterations, and epigenetic alterations among others. Research over recent years has revealed many strong associations, but much is still unknown. Once prostate cancer develops, the most important thing is early detection.
