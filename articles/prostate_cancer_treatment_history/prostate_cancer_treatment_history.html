The History of Prostate Cancer Treatment

Cole Ruoff · October 10, 2026

Introduction

Throughout the history of prostate cancer oncology, there have been multiple scientific discoveries and key clinical trials that define the progress made in treating the disease. Additionally, the treatment of prostate cancer has progressed from just addressing patient pain and symptoms to curative approaches in modern day. This post will outline the advancements and progress made in prostate cancer treatment.

History of Prostate Cancer Treatment

The first historical record describing the prostate was likely from the Greek Herophilus of Chalcedon in the third century BC1,2. The discovery of the prostate is typically attributed to Niccolò Massa, a Venetian physician who described the prostate in his 1536 work Anatomiae liber introductorius2. The first case of prostate cancer leading to death was observed in 1822 by a German physician, S. Beling3. In 1904, Hugh Hampton Young performed the first radical prostatectomy through the transperineal approach4. Other attempts to cure prostate cancer involved early forms of radiotherapy. In 1909, Henri Minet shared his results from using tubes containing radium that were inserted via urethral or suprapubic catheters4. Additionally, Hugh Hampton Young attempted similar approaches for curing prostate cancer4. Ultimately, these approaches were difficult and painful for the patients, so this early form of brachytherapy5 did not gain traction until relatively recently4. Decades later in 1941, Charles Huggins and Clarence V. Hodges reported that castration or estrogen injections delayed the progression of metastatic prostate cancer, while testosterone injections sped it up6. Huggins later received the Nobel Prize in 1966 for this work. These findings emphasized the hormone dependence of prostate cancer7.

It wasn’t until the 1950s that X-ray machines capable of producing radiation that could penetrate deep tissue and reach the prostate were developed. Before this, external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) with X-ray machines was only used for more easily accessible malignancies4. After this technology was developed, the “megavoltage era” of radiotherapy took place, which involved the use of linear accelerators to produce radiation to target prostate tumors8. In 1965, Bagshaw et al.9 published their findings from 81 patients with localized prostate cancer who were treated with EBRT, which resulted in a 5-year survival rate of 54%. Due to this study and others at the time, EBRT became an acceptable treatment modality for prostate cancer and remained so for decades, with later developments such as intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) building on this foundation10-12. In the 1970s, advances in brachytherapy technologies revived the interest in the field. Basil Hilaris and Willet Whitmore Jr. at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center developed a method to implant iodine-125 isotopes which did not require any imaging guidance4,5. This method was eventually found to be extremely limited by long-term failure and other complications. In the 1980s, Hans Henrik Holm from Denmark developed transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) guided brachytherapy approaches which enabled the treatment modality to remain in clinical practice for decades4,5.

From the 1970s through the 1980s, more discoveries about the hormones that drive prostate cancer were made. This added an additional dimension to the treatment ecosystem of prostate cancer oncology. In the early 1970s, Andrew V. Schally’s group from Tulane University School of Medicine discovered the structure of luteinizing hormone releasing hormone (LHRH)13. This discovery along with methods for synthesizing LHRH led to Schally being awarded a Nobel Prize in 19774. A study in 1978 by Sandow et al.14 later demonstrated that treating rats with LHRH agonists suppressed testosterone production following an initial surge. This led Schally’s group and others to demonstrate that using this LHRH agonist treatment approach in patients led to patient benefit in 198215. In the mid-1980s, buserelin and leuprolide were the first LHRH agonists approved for use in prostate cancer patients16. Still to this day, LHRH agonists are one of the most used forms of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT)17 and are now referred to as gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists.

The discovery of the androgen receptor itself was in 1968, which led to many research groups searching for a way to target it therapeutically18. Initial attempts at developing antiandrogens like cyproterone were unsuccessful due to off-target effects in the brain and testicles4. Adding an acetate group produced cyproterone acetate, but it was never approved by the FDA for prostate cancer. Instead, the nonsteroidal antiandrogen flutamide became the first antiandrogen approved by the FDA for the treatment of prostate cancer in 19894. Pursuing androgen deprivation approaches was especially important in prostate cancer due to its strong reliance on androgens and the fact that there was almost no benefit from chemotherapies until relatively recently.

In 1981, there was the first sign of chemotherapy showing potential benefit for prostate cancer. Estramustine displayed improved biochemical recurrence-free survival, but did not improve overall survival19 and had limiting side effects20. In a 1996 study of patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, mitoxantrone was shown to improve palliative endpoints but still did not improve overall survival21. Later, two landmark trials in 2004, SWOG 99-1622 and TAX-32723, proved that docetaxel improved overall survival, either in combination with estramustine or with prednisone. Then in 2015, the CHAARTED trial showed that docetaxel also improved patient outcomes in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer24. Besides docetaxel, cabazitaxel is the only other chemotherapy agent that has shown improved OS in metastatic prostate cancer25. Both of these agents are taxanes, and their cytotoxic mechanism of action involves stabilizing microtubules26.

Recent advances in ADT include LHRH antagonists, which include abarelix, degarelix, and relugolix. Abarelix was withdrawn shortly after approval due to concerns of hypersensitivity27, but degarelix and relugolix still remain available28. A main benefit of LHRH antagonists, compared to LHRH agonists, is that they do not induce the initial flare up which leads to intense side effects in some patients28. In the 2000s, a new form of ADT was developed, androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs). These agents have been shown to improve survival in mCRPC, mHSPC, and nonmetastatic CRPC28. Some of these ARPIs include abiraterone acetate, enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide28. Second-generation ARPIs, such as enzalutamide, apalutamide and darolutamide, have the same mechanism of action as first-generation antiandrogens but were improved to have much higher binding affinity, higher selectivity, and more complete suppression of AR-driven transcription7,29. Abiraterone, which blocks androgen synthesis, was approved in 2011 and enzalutamide in 2012, both initially for mCRPC after docetaxel19. Recently in 2022, darolutamide was the first ARPI to display improved survival outcomes in combination with docetaxel and ADT in mHSPC30.

Beyond advancing hormonal therapy, it is now recommended for metastatic prostate cancer patients to receive genetic testing when possible28,31. This is because carriers of BRCA1, BRCA2, and ATM mutations had improved radiographic progression-free survival, and later improved overall survival, when treated with the poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitor olaparib in the 2020 PROfound trial32,33. Despite the success in immunotherapy in multiple other cancer types, prostate cancer treatment has not seen the same advances. Common immunotherapy approaches such as pembrolizumab have been tested in clinical trials but none have displayed robust improvements in patient outcomes34. The only FDA approved immunotherapy developed specifically for prostate cancer is a cancer vaccine for mCRPC, sipuleucel-T35. Although used in the clinic, the overall efficacy remains limited.

History of Prostate Cancer Surgery

While advancements were being made in prostate cancer treatment agents, surgical intervention was improving in parallel. The origins of prostate cancer surgery began with the radical prostatectomy, which involved removal of the entire prostate along with the seminal vesicles. In the early 20th century, Hugh Hampton Young performed one of the first reported transperineal open radical prostatectomies4,36. Then in the mid-20th century, Millin37 introduced the retropubic approach which enabled more complete pelvic lymph node dissection. A very important advancement to radical prostatectomy was made in 1982 when Walsh and Donker introduced the nerve-sparing technique. This immensely improved efficacy and increased the percentage of patients who retained sexual function and urinary continence after surgery38. The late 20th century brought about the laparoscopic radical prostatectomy. This minimally invasive approach involves performing the removal of the prostate via small incisions made on the patient’s abdomen39. This led to fewer complications and faster recovery40,41. Further advancements to prostate cancer surgery include the introduction of robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (RALP)42. This approach utilizes robotic arms and instruments that enhance three-dimensional visualization and improve dexterity43,44. Additionally, RALP is associated with less blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and faster patient recovery45–47. However, despite its benefits and technological advancements, various studies have shown no significant differences in oncological control or functional outcomes with RALP compared to open surgery45–47. RALP also increases costs which adds to the economic burden of cancer treatment48. RALP is a major advancement to minimally invasive surgery but due to its recent introduction more time is needed to fully determine its long-term benefits. A recent extension of RALP is long-distance RALP or telesurgery49. This approach involves remote operation of the robotic systems that perform the prostatectomy. Telesurgery’s main advantage is providing care and reducing medical disparities across the globe. With this approach, all that is needed is an internet connection and surgical equipment, then surgeons can remotely perform potentially life-saving operations. This is not without its downsides of course. Aspects that currently limit remote surgery include communication latency and reliability and real-time safety systems.

Conclusion

Throughout the evolution of prostate cancer treatment, there have been great advancements in both therapies and surgery. Along with the progress in this aspect of prostate cancer oncology, diagnostics have similarly advanced.
