What is the Prostate?

Cole Ruoff · September 4, 2026

Introduction

The prostate is a small accessory gland in the male reproductive system that is present in all mammals1,21,2. It sits just below the bladder and wraps around the top of the urethra3,4. In humans it's roughly the size of a walnut and is made up of glandular tissue, smooth muscle, and connective tissue1,4. In normal human physiology, the prostate performs several distinct functions in the body, from producing prostatic fluid, a key component of semen, to physically regulating the switch between urination and ejaculation5,6.

The Function

The primary role of the prostate is to produce and secrete prostatic fluid, which constitutes about 25-30% of the total semen volume7. This fluid contains multiple components including zinc which serves to stabilize the DNA found in sperm cells and various enzymes that support sperm survival, transport, and fertilization1,8. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and other proteases enable the liquefaction of semen after ejaculation, thereby allowing sperm to swim more freely9,10. This function of the prostate is just one step of the coordinated process that produces semen but the specialized compounds in the prostatic fluid protect sperm and increases the likelihood of successful fertilization1. Beyond its role in producing prostatic fluid, the prostate also functions as a mechanical switch between urination and ejaculation11,12. This is driven by two muscle systems running adjacent and parallel to the prostatic urethra, the urethral dilator (musculus dilatator urethrae) and musculus ejaculatorius11. The coordinated activation of these muscles alters the shape of the prostate which facilitates the switch between ejaculation and urination states11,13,14. During urination, contraction of the urethral dilator muscle shortens the prostatic urethra and alters the orientation and shape of the prostate. As a result, the prostatic urethral lumen widens, facilitating the passage of urine from the bladder into the urethra11,13,14. During ejaculation, sympathetic activation initiates a coordinated contractile response involving the musculus ejaculatorius and smooth muscle of the reproductive tract, including the vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate7,15. Simultaneously, contraction of the bladder neck prevents retrograde ejaculation into the bladder7. Prostatic and ejaculatory duct contractions then deliver seminal fluid into the urethra, while coordinated pelvic floor contractions propel semen distally through the penile urethra7,16. The prostate also functions to control semen release via coordinated smooth-muscle cell contractions that compress the glandular ducts of the prostate which pushes prostatic fluid into the urethra and helps mix prostatic secretions with sperm and seminal vesicle fluid17,18. This also contributes to the forward movement of semen through the urethra7. Additionally, the prostate contributes to prevent infections of the urinary and reproductive tracts19–21. Through the urethra, the prostate is exposed to the outside world and encounters bacteria which is dealt with through the prostate’s built-in defense mechanisms. High accumulations of zinc in the prostate provide a natural antimicrobial environment8. Zinc contributes to antimicrobial defense by inhibiting bacterial enzymes, disrupting bacterial metabolism, and limiting overall bacterial growth22–24. There are also immune cells present in the prostate which enable immune surveillance and the detection of infections25–27.

Anatomy

The prostate is a pyramid-shaped organ located below the bladder28. The base of the prostate sits against the bladder with the apex facing downwards28. A normal prostate weighs about 15-20g28. The prostate can be divided into four zones or regions: the peripheral zone (PZ), the central zone (CZ), the transition zone (TZ), and the fibromuscular region4,29. The peripheral zone makes up >70% of the prostate glandular tissue, with the central zone and transition zone making up ~25% and ~5%, respectively4. The fibromuscular region sits at the front of the prostate and contains muscle and fibrous tissue instead of glandular cells4. The peripheral zone is typically where prostate cancer develops with about 70-80% of cancer originating from this zone29. Prostate cancer can arise from the central and transition zones but occurs at lower rates29. The transition zone grows throughout life and this causes the disease of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)30. The prostate is a branched tubuloalveolar exocrine gland, meaning it contains secretory units (acini) and branching ducts that function to produce prostatic fluid31,32. The acini are small pouch-shaped glandular units lining a central lumen and are responsible for producing and secreting prostatic fluid into the lumen28. Once this fluid is secreted into the lumen it drains through the system of branching epithelial ducts and tubules which all converge to the prostatic urethra28. Together, the acini and ducts make up the prostate epithelium, which is traditionally classified into three types of epithelial cells: luminal, basal, and neuroendocrine28. The luminal epithelial cells are terminally differentiated specialized secretory epithelial cells33. They have a columnar shape and line the lumen of the prostate33. These cells express markers specific to luminal epithelial cells including cytokeratin (CK) CK8 and CK18, AR, and AR targets like KLK333. The primary function of the luminal cells is to produce the proteins and compounds that comprise prostatic fluid33. The basal epithelial cells are found between the luminal epithelial cells and adjacent to the basement membrane33. These cells provide structural support to the tissue by anchoring luminal epithelial cells to the basement membrane33. They also function as progenitor cells to regenerate the glandular epithelium after an injury33. Basal cells have a flattened morphology and distinct cell markers33. These markers include CK5, CK14, TP63, and very low to no AR expression33. Due to this low expression of AR, these cells do not need androgens to survive and grow33. The neuroendocrine epithelial cells are found rarely dispersed among the luminal and basal epithelial cells34. They are reported to make up about 1% of the cells in the epithelium34. The function of neuroendocrine cells is to sense pH and chemical changes and regulate growth via various growth factors as they have been found to have a secretory function34. They have an elongated morphology, lack AR expression, and express neuroendocrine-lineage markers such as chromogranin A (CHGA), synaptophysin (SYP), and neuron-specific enolase (NSE)34. More recent research has identified additional epithelial cell types beyond the traditional categories33. There have been multiple reports of mixed cell types where cells express markers of more than one cell type (e.g. luminal and basal markers)33. Additionally, there have been non-canonical epithelial cell types identified in the human prostate that resemble the morphology and transcriptomic profiles of club and hillock cells of the respiratory tract33. The exact roles of these cells in the prostate are still largely unknown but new research suggests they play a similar role of immune modulation and lineage plasticity33. The stroma of the prostate is fibromuscular, and it includes fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, nerves, and assorted infiltrating cells (e.g., mast cells, lymphocytes)35. There is no adipose tissue in the prostate28. The epithelial cells in the prostate receive nutrients and hormones through the stroma cells36. Cellular communication among epithelial cells and stromal cells plays a strong role in regulated function in the prostate35,36.

Conclusion

The prostate is a complex organ that plays an important role in male urinary and sexual function37. Across the world, prostate cancer is one of the most common cancer types38. In the next post, I will discuss prostate cancer etiology and tumorigenesis.
